Briefing

Before you read another headline

Most of what reaches you today wasn't written to inform you first — it was written to earn your click, your feeling, and your share. That's not always a lie. But it means the words in front of you have been shaped, and it's worth learning to see the shaping.

This is a short field exercise, not a quiz. You won't be graded on what you already know. Instead you'll look at two real-feeling pieces of media, make your own honest call, and then see exactly how they were built — the techniques, the target, and the truth underneath.

Why this matters You make dozens of split-second trust decisions a day — what to click, believe, and pass along. The gap between what something claims and what it actually shows is where most misinformation lives. Learning to spot that gap is a skill, and like any skill, it gets sharper with practice.

Exhibit A · Headline Detective

Headlines are written to be clicked, not summarized

Publishers earn attention, not accuracy. So a single small study becomes "scientists prove," a disagreement becomes "chaos," and "could happen eventually" becomes "will happen by next year." The headline promises something specific — your job is to check whether the story actually delivers it.

Why this matters Studies on news sharing consistently find that a large share of people share articles after reading only the headline. If the headline oversells the story, that exaggeration is often the only version of the "news" that ever spreads.

Next: you'll see a real-feeling headline and article. Decide honestly whether you'd click it — then mark the words that feel exaggerated.

Exhibit B · Emotion Detector

A feeling can arrive before a thought does

Fear, anger, and urgency spread faster than calm explanation because they're built to make you react before you reflect. A post doesn't need to lie outright — it just needs the right words in the right order to make you feel something strongly enough to stop scrolling, and start sharing.

Why this matters The emotion a post triggers in you is rarely an accident — it's frequently the entire design goal, chosen because it drives clicks, comments, or purchases. Naming the emotion and the exact words that caused it puts a half-second of thought back between the post and your reaction.
